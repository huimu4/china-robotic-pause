From Line to Shop Floor: XPENG's IRON Starts Work at the Guangzhou Auto Show, Mass Production Set for January 2027

On November 27, eight IRON units opened the Guangzhou auto show as receptionists, guides and product presenters — the first time XPENG's humanoid has faced the public in numbers. Two days earlier, at AI Day, the company fixed the commercial clock: hardware mass production in January 2027, store trials in Q1, commercial release in Q2, global launch in H2 2027. The robot has a job. The harder question is whether the factory, the data and the buyers arrive on the same schedule.

By China Robotics Insider · Sep 11, 2026 · Last updated: Nov 27, 2026 · 12 min read
Share on X LinkedIn
The answer · TL;DR
XPENG has moved IRON from a line-side milestone to a customer-facing timetable. At AI Day on November 25, 2026 the company said hardware mass production starts in January 2027, the robot begins a store-assistant trial in Q1, goes on formal sale to business customers in Q2, and launches globally in H2 2027. On November 27, eight IRON units worked the Guangzhou auto show as reception, guide and product staff. The hardware claim is 76 degrees of freedom including a 21-DOF hand, 1,500+ tactile points and 2,250 TOPS from three in-house Turing chips, all running on-device. The manufacturing claim is a line with 600+ AI inspection checkpoints, 1,000+ vehicle-grade tests and a 2.5-minute takt at full capacity — a theoretical ~210,000 units a year, not a forecast. The market's initial verdict was a shrug: XPENG's US shares closed 0.33% lower on AI Day.

Humanoid announcements have a tell. If the robot only walks, bows and waves, the company is selling a video. If the robot is given a rota, a badge and a script, the company is selling a product. At the Guangzhou auto show this week, XPENG gave eight IRON units the second kind of job: they stood at the booth in hall 17.2, greeted visitors, walked them through the stand, explained the cars and, in one case, delivered the speech. It is still theatre — a trade fair is a controlled environment — but it is theatre with a schedule attached, and the schedule is what matters.

The commercial calendar XPENG published at AI Day is unusually specific for this industry: hardware mass production in January 2027; a store-assistant trial in selected XPENG retail stores in the first quarter; a formal release to business customers in the second quarter; a global launch in the second half of the year. For a sector where "next year" has been a rolling promise since 2023, a January date is a commitment someone can check.

What actually changed since September

This page started as a report on the September 8 commissioning of XPENG's humanoid assembly line, when the first IRON completed final assembly with more than 80% of core processes automated and walked off the line under its own power. That was a manufacturing story: proof that a car company could put a general-purpose humanoid through an automotive-grade process rather than a lab bench.

The November events change the subject. AI Day 2026, held under the theme "Origin", was about the product and the business model, not the line. XPENG introduced IRON as a "work partner" built around five priorities — foundational capability, safety, emotional value, 24/7 availability and job performance — and spent most of the presentation on what the robot can do in a role rather than what it can do in a demo.

Then came the show. Eight units is the largest public deployment XPENG has attempted, and the tasks were deliberately mundane: reception, wayfinding, vehicle explanation. Mundane is the point. A robot that can hold a conversation about trim levels and trunk volume for six hours is closer to a retail product than one that can do a backflip.

The internet, naturally, found the comedy. A clip circulating on November 27 showed CEO He Xiaopeng greeting one of the units with "good work, little Iron" and reporting that the robot kept calling him "Lei Jun" — the Xiaomi founder whose booth sat across the aisle. XPENG has not commented on the mix-up. It is a reminder that the perception stack is still a work in progress, and that a trade show is a much noisier data set than a store.

The commercial timetable — and the one date that moved

XPENG's November 27 statement and its November 25 release agree on the structure but not on every label. The company's own sequence is:

MilestoneDateWhat it means
Hardware mass productionJanuary 2027The Guangzhou line switches from commissioning to series build. Not the same as volume shipments.
Store-assistant trialQ1 2027IRON works in selected XPENG stores: reception, product guidance, retail service. Paid deployment inside the company's own network.
Commercial releaseQ2 2027Formal sale to business customers. XPENG's Nov 27 wording; a Nov 25 Xinhua/Sina report said February for the public launch.
Global launchH2 2027International markets, including markets where XPENG already sells cars.

Sources: XPENG AI Day release (Nov 25, 2026); XPENG Guangzhou auto show release (Nov 27, 2026); Xinhua Finance via Sina (Nov 25, 2026). Where the two company statements differ, the later one is treated as current.

The February-versus-Q2 discrepancy is worth flagging rather than smoothing over. Chinese corporate calendars often use "发布" for a product unveiling and "交付" for the first customer delivery; a February unveiling and a Q2 commercial release are not necessarily contradictory, but they are not the same promise either. Anyone modelling revenue off this should use Q2, and should assume the first paid units are few.

What IRON actually is

The November release is more precise than the September one, and it corrects a number that circulated widely in the first wave of coverage. IRON does not have 76 body degrees of freedom plus 21 per hand; it has 76 degrees of freedom in total, including a 21-degree-of-freedom dexterous hand. That is a meaningful difference for anyone comparing it with Unitree or Tesla hardware.

The decision to run the models on-device is the most consequential engineering choice in the spec sheet. A robot that depends on a cloud link is a robot that stops working when the Wi-Fi does; a robot that carries its model is more expensive per unit and far more defensible in a retail store, a factory aisle or a hotel lobby. XPENG is paying that cost deliberately, and it is the same bet it makes in cars.

The factory behind the robot

The September line is now the supply side of the January date. XPENG's own description: a purpose-built automated humanoid line, more than 600 AI-powered holographic inspection checkpoints, more than 1,000 vehicle-grade tests on complete robots and key components, and a designed takt of one IRON every 2.5 minutes at full capacity.

Do the arithmetic and the theoretical ceiling is about 210,000 units a year, running 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, with no changeovers, no rework and no downtime. Nobody in this industry hits that. A realistic first-year output is a small percentage of it, set by orders, shift patterns and yield. The number is useful for one thing only: it tells you the line was specified for volume, not for a photo opportunity.

The other half of the infrastructure is data. XPENG's robot data factory covers 50,000 m², more than 250 scenarios and 15 job categories, and reuses the data processing and evaluation tools built for its cars. That is the quiet advantage of a car company entering robotics: it already owns a data pipeline, a quality organisation and a supplier base, and it can point all three at a new product without building them from zero.

XPENG puts the supply-chain overlap with its EV plants at roughly 85%. If that holds, the marginal cost of a humanoid programme is much lower than for a standalone robotics startup — and the ramp is limited by demand and reliability rather than by whether someone can source a harmonic reducer.

Why a carmaker thinks it can sell this

Three assets sit behind the timetable. The first is money: in August 2026 XPENG Robotics closed a first external round of more than US$900 million at a valuation above US$6.3 billion, reported as one of the largest private rounds in embodied AI. The second is distribution: XPENG already operates a retail network and a service organisation, so the first "customer" for IRON is its own store estate, which is the cheapest possible place to discover that a robot cannot handle a queue of three people asking about financing.

The third is a research footprint that reaches beyond Guangzhou. At the same show XPENG announced a Hong Kong R&D centre in the Hong Kong Science Park, focused on robotics and general intelligence, with a stated division of labour across Hong Kong, Shenzhen and Guangzhou. That is a talent play as much as a technology one — Hong Kong gives the company international hiring and university partnerships that mainland-only labs find harder to assemble.

The business model is still forming. XPENG describes hardware sales plus software subscriptions plus an open robotics ecosystem in which partners download capability packages, customise skills and validate them for their own sites. It is also exploring token-based subscriptions and scenario-based services — a way to keep charging after the robot ships, and an admission that the margin is in the skills, not the metal.

The market's verdict: a shrug, for now

XPENG's US-listed shares closed at US$8.99 on November 25, down 0.33% on the day of AI Day, after a 3.9% gain the previous session. That is not a rejection; it is the market treating a humanoid timetable as a 2027 story in a quarter that is still being judged on car deliveries and margins.

It is also a useful corrective to the enthusiasm around the show. Eight robots greeting journalists is a deployment in the way a pop-up shop is a retail chain. The questions that will move the stock are duller: how many units ship in Q2, at what price, with what uptime, and how much of the revenue is recurring software rather than one-off hardware.

Against the rest of the field, IRON is neither the cheapest nor the most shipped. Unitree and AgiBot already move thousands of humanoids a year, much of it into research, education and showroom work; UBTECH has its own industrial and service lines. XPENG's differentiation is the car-grade quality system and the retail channel, which is a credible answer to the enterprise buyer's real objection: not "can it do a trick" but "will it still be working in month eighteen".

What it does not yet have is proof at scale. A line that can make 210,000 units a year and a plan to sell into your own stores are two different capabilities, and the gap between them is where most humanoid programmes have died. The January date is the first honest test.

What to watch

  • January 2027, not the press release. Whether the line actually enters series build, and whether XPENG discloses units built rather than "capacity".
  • The Q1 store trial in numbers. How many stores, how many robots, how many hours per day, and whether the robots are supervised. An unsupervised eight-hour shift is a different product from a scripted demo.
  • Q2 pricing. The company has hinted at pricing "near EV levels" in press coverage without giving a figure. The first list price will tell you whether this is a capital good or a marketing asset.
  • Recurring revenue share. How much of the commercial model is subscription and token-based service versus hardware. That is the difference between a robot company and a car company with a robot.
  • The perception gap. Public misidentifications, like the "Lei Jun" clip, are minor; repeated ones in a paid deployment would not be.

Frequently asked questions

What did XPENG's IRON robots do at the Guangzhou Auto Show?

Eight IRON units made their first public show appearance at XPENG's booth in hall 17.2 of the 24th Guangzhou International Automobile Exhibition, which opened on November 27, 2026. They worked reception, guided visitors around the stand and explained vehicle features, and one unit delivered a speech on stage. XPENG framed the appearance as on-the-job training for retail roles, not a commercial deployment.

When does XPENG IRON go into mass production and on sale?

Hardware mass production is scheduled to begin in January 2027. In Q1 2027 the robot starts a store-assistant trial in selected XPENG retail stores. XPENG's November 27 statement targets a formal release to business customers in Q2 2027, followed by a global launch in the second half of 2027. A November 25 Xinhua/Sina report quoted February for the public launch; the later company statement is treated as current.

What are the XPENG IRON specifications?

XPENG's November 25 release lists 76 degrees of freedom including a 21-degree-of-freedom dexterous hand, more than 1,500 tactile sensing points, 3D vision with 360-degree perception, three in-house Turing AI chips delivering 2,250 TOPS of effective compute, and the IRON VLM multimodal system running on-device. Height and weight were not disclosed in that release.

How many IRON robots can XPENG's factory build?

XPENG says its automated humanoid line, with more than 600 AI-powered holographic inspection checkpoints and over 1,000 vehicle-grade tests, is designed to complete one IRON every 2.5 minutes at full capacity. Run flat out, that is a theoretical ceiling of roughly 210,000 units a year, though actual output will follow orders and shift patterns.

Is IRON a competitor to Tesla's Optimus or Unitree?

It competes in the same humanoid market but from a different starting point. XPENG is a carmaker using an estimated 85% supply-chain overlap with its EV plants, automotive-grade quality gates and a 50,000 m² robot data factory. Unitree and AgiBot already ship thousands of units a year at lower price points; IRON is aimed at service roles where consistency and uptime matter more than sticker price.
