Development history

Four Decades, One Ascent

China went from zero domestic robots in 1980 to the world's largest robotics market — and now the world's largest humanoid-robot manufacturer. Here is how it happened, in six acts.

1970s – 1980s · The Seeds

A lone scientist plants the flag

In 1972, Jiang Xinsong of the Shenyang Institute of Automation made what was then a fringe proposal: that China should build robots. Eight years later, in 1980, his team completed China's first industrial robot — a teach-and-playback manipulator. At a time when few in the country took robotics seriously, Jiang's stubbornness earned him the title that sticks today: "Father of Chinese Robotics."

1986 – 1999 · The 863 Program

National will meets the lab

In 1986, China launched the 863 Program, a national high-tech research plan that made robotics a strategic priority. Jiang Xinsong served as chief scientist of the automation domain. Under its umbrella, Chinese teams completed the PJ-1 painting robot (1990), developed seven series of industrial robots with proprietary IP, ran more than 100 application projects and built nine industrialisation bases. By the 1990s, research capability was genuinely world-class — the missing piece was a market.

2000 – 2013 · Industrialisation

China's first robotics company is born

In April 2000, a team led by Qu Daokui — a protégé of Jiang Xinsong — spun out of the Shenyang Institute of Automation to found SIASUN, named in honour of the late Jiang. It was China's first dedicated robot company. Over the next decade, SIASUN and peers such as Estun (founded 1993) turned research into products, while the country's booming automotive and electronics factories created an endless stream of demand. In 2013, China overtook Japan to become the world's largest industrial-robot market — a position it has never relinquished.

2013 – 2020 · The Robot-Replacement Boom

"Machine replacing man" reshapes a nation

In 2014, Dongguan — the heart of the "world's factory" — became the first city to launch a "robot replacement" (机器换人) initiative. That same year, HKUST professor Li Zexiang founded XbotPark in Songshan Lake, an incubator that would spawn more than 80 hardware companies and several unicorns, with DJI as its crown jewel. Meanwhile consumer robotics took off: Ecovacs and Roborock turned Chinese homes into robot vacuums' largest market. The era's defining shift: China stopped just buying robots and started building its own ecosystem.

2020 – 2023 · The Humanoid Spark

Tesla's Optimus lights the fuse

Tesla's Optimus reveal in 2022 poured petrol on the global humanoid race — and China reacted fastest. 2023 was the inflection year: Agibot was founded by Huawei "genius" Peng Zhihui in Shanghai; Fourier shipped its GR-1 humanoid; UBTECH became the world's first listed humanoid-robot company on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in December. More than 47 Chinese humanoid startups were now chasing the same prize.

2024 – Now · Mass Production & The Great Shakeout

From demo to factory floor

2025 was the crossing point: domestic brands overtook foreign brands in China's industrial-robot market for the first time, and China shipped roughly 18,000 humanoid robots — the majority of the global total. Unitree filed for a Star Market IPO in a record 73 days; Agibot followed toward Hong Kong. 2026 is widely called the "year of mass production," but also the year of consolidation — the capital that once backed 50 players is concentrating on five to eight. The industry has entered its most consequential act yet.
