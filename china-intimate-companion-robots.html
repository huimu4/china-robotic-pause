Inside China's Intimate-Robot Industry: Doll Makers, AI Companions, and the Regulation Gap

China's adult-oriented realistic dolls have long been a manufacturing export business. The 2026 change is the layer on top: servo-driven heads, voice LLMs, saved memory and tactile sensors bolted onto a TPE and silicone supply chain that already supplies most of the world's dolls. This piece maps the makers, the technology stack, the price gap with US benchmarks, and the regulation and ethics still catching up.

By China Robotics Insider · Nov 24, 2026 · 10 min read
Share on X LinkedIn
The short version
China hosts the world's largest cluster of realistic adult-doll makers, concentrated in Zhongshan and Dongguan in Guangdong, with TPE and silicone bodies, articulated alloy skeletons and a long export history. The newest wave adds robotics and AI: servo faces, LLM voice chat with saved memory, preset personalities and tactile sensors. The best-documented AI line is WMdoll's MetaBox, launched overseas in early 2025 at US$1,600–2,000 with high-end models near US$3,000 and an optional AI subscription; EXDOLL, founded in 2009, is the older high-end silicone and 3D-printing house. The US benchmark Realbotix (RealDoll/Harmony) lists full robots near US$11,350. In China, adult products are legal when compliant, but sexualized minor forms are illegal; a Guangdong group standard on classification and labeling was initiated in December 2025, and July 2026 AI companion-service rules cover the chat layer. Most Chinese AI dolls currently sell overseas first; domestic marketing and regulation remain a gray zone.

There are two adjacent robotics stories that often get mixed together. One is the companion-robot boom — robot pets, plush emotional toys and full-size reception or care androids. The other is industrial and service humanoids built for factories, malls and hospitals. This piece sits in a third, quieter lane: adult-oriented realistic dolls, and the robotics and AI being added to them. The line between them matters because the buyers, the regulation and the product expectations are different.

What this category actually is

Products in this lane stack in three layers, and most makers sell more than one.

The base layer is a static realistic doll: a TPE or silicone body over an articulated metal or alloy skeleton, with interchangeable faces, wigs and outfits. No electronics, no motion, no conversation. This is the product that has made Guangdong an export base for decades.

The second layer adds robotics: a servo-driven head and neck, eye, brow and mouth movement, optional heating and tactile sensors that respond to touch. The body still relies on the articulated skeleton; the robot part is concentrated in the face, neck and, on some models, a few limb joints.

The third layer is the AI companion software: LLM voice conversation, memory that carries across sessions, switchable personality presets, multilingual speech, and an app or subscription that manages the character. On several lines the AI runs through a phone app rather than on board the doll, so the hardware is partly a physical shell for a voice model.

Where China sits in the doll supply base

The physical supply chain is where China's position is strongest. TPE polymer and medical-grade silicone come from domestic chemical suppliers; skeletons are alloy or stainless steel with articulating joints; faces and bodies sit in a large library of molds, and custom looks can be tuned to regional aesthetics. 3D scanning and 3D printing are used for likeness and custom-body work. Electronics — servo boards, sensors, speakers, Bluetooth pairing — sit in the nearby Pearl River Delta component ecosystem.

The production cluster is concentrated around Zhongshan and Dongguan in Guangdong, with raw materials sourced from the Yangtze River delta and electronics assembled locally. A 36Kr industry report, citing industry estimates, puts China at roughly 70% of global silicone-doll capacity. That figure is a third-party industry estimate, not an audited export statistic; treat it as a directional indicator of how concentrated the manufacturing base is.

Price compression is the other Chinese effect. High-end Japanese dolls historically sold in the RMB 80,000–100,000 band; Chinese entrants pushed static products into the thousand-dollar range while adding mold and finish options. 36Kr reports that 47-year-old Japanese maker Orient Industry closed in 2024, a shift the trade press attributes partly to cost pressure from Chinese supply. Most Chinese makers remain export-first: cross-border listings, OEM and ODM work for overseas brands, and AI lines that launched in North America, Japan and Europe before a domestic Chinese-language version was ready.

The named makers

WMdoll (金三玩美), Zhongshan. Per Tencent News, the brand traces to 2010 and started out making clothing mannequins before moving into realistic dolls. Its MetaBox robotic AI series began attracting overseas attention from February 2025. China Daily reported on March 25, 2025 that the first 200-unit batch sold out in North America, Japan and Europe at US$1,600–2,000, with high-end models around US$3,000; the dolls used LLM voice conversation, 32 wireless tactile sensors, eight personality and voice presets, and conversation in English, German, Japanese, Korean, French and Spanish. A full-function AI subscription started at around US$100 for the first year, and a Chinese-language version was in development at the time. One corporate caveat: the Zhongshan operating entity listed under the WMdoll brand entered deregistration proceedings in January 2026, and WMDOLL and GALATEA trademarks were transferred in 2025; the current corporate entity behind the brand should be verified directly with the maker.

EXDOLL. Per its official site, founded in 2009, EXDOLL is one of the older full-size realistic-doll houses in China. Its product lines span multiple series including Utopia, Ukiyo-e, Clone and Cyberfusion; the company describes medical-grade silicone, an alloy skeleton, 3D-scan data collection and 3D-printed cloning, and lists itself as a national high-tech enterprise. Its positioning leans toward collectible and high-end craft rather than the cheapest export band.

The wider cluster. Chinese trade press names other Zhongshan and Dongguan makers including Chunshuitang, Junying and IronTech Doll (Zhongshan, founded 2015), alongside smaller producers in Shandong and Shenzhen that sell OEM or ODM units under private labels on B2B platforms. The catalog is fragmented, brand names shift between domestic and export registrations, and most product detail pages are adult-oriented; this overview describes product tiers and supply chain rather than product functions.

What the AI layer actually does

On the hardware side, an AI-equipped doll adds a servo head with degrees of freedom for the eyes, brows, mouth and neck; tactile sensors across the face or body; optional heating; and app pairing over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi. Some demos show an add-on board that upgrades an existing doll with vision, touch perception and local inference, but those capabilities come mainly from vendor demonstrations and have not been independently verified.

On the software side, the layer combines a voice LLM, long-context memory across sessions, preset personalities, multilingual speech, and sometimes mood or relationship state. The model can be third-party or vendor-built; what the maker discloses about whether conversation runs in the cloud or on device varies. That last point is the main privacy question: conversation history, stored preferences and any sensor or camera data may live on a phone, a maker server, or a third-party model endpoint, and the disclosure is often thin.

The economics are hardware plus a recurring AI subscription. The physical doll and the software character are sold separately in several product lines, so the price headline usually covers the doll while the conversation, memory and personality features renew on a plan.

Market size: two different numbers

Readers comparing market figures should keep two very different categories apart.

The narrow AI-doll segment: QYResearch, cited by 36Kr, estimated the global AI silicone-doll market at about RMB 2.32 billion in 2024, approaching RMB 7.05 billion by 2031 at a 17.3% CAGR. This is a third-party research estimate for a narrow, fast-moving niche, not an audited revenue figure.

The broad adult-products market: iiMedia Research puts China's broader adult-products market at RMB 208.127 billion in 2025, up 7.2% year on year, with a forecast of RMB 277.14 billion by 2030. That total includes toys, care products and devices far beyond dolls; it should not be read as a doll or robot segment size. There is no audited domestic size specific to Chinese AI dolls, and because domestic sale and marketing operate under restricted rules, most of the visible reported volume is export-oriented.

The US benchmark: Realbotix and RealDoll

Realbotix Corp grew out of Abyss Creations, the San Diego company behind the RealDoll line; it trades under TSXV: XBOT, OTC: XBOTF and the Frankfurt listing 76M0. The founder and sculptor is Matt McMullen; the current CEO is Andrew Kiguel. Its Harmony AI system pairs a servo head with personality traits and conversation memory, and its Aria model uses a 17-motor silicone face. Dealer snapshots collected in September 2026 listed a robotic head at about US$7,349.99 and a full robot at about US$11,349.99, with some distributors showing backorders into October 2026.

The structural gap is about five to seven times on price. The US side sells a handcrafted object, a mature AI companion brand and a dealer service network; the Chinese side competes on the physical supply chain — materials, molds, assembly cost and the breadth of looks and options. Software maturity, brand trust and after-sales are where the US benchmark still leads.

One cross-border data point from 36Kr: Berlin's Cybrothel, an AI doll venue, charges around €249 per night and reportedly sources more durable models from Chinese suppliers. The report treats the venue and supplier relationship as media-reported; it is not a disclosed supply contract.

Regulation and ethics in China

The legal baseline. Adult products may be produced and sold in China when they comply with applicable rules. What is strictly prohibited is any sexualized depiction of minors: the Minors Protection Law bars production, holding or distribution of obscene materials involving minors, and criminal obscenity statutes apply. Late-2025 reports exposed dolls marketed with child-like features; a Huizhou district joint investigation paused production and ran a sweep of related listings, per Legal Daily reporting.

Classification and labeling. On December 9, 2025, the Guangdong Sexology Society initiated a group standard on adult-oriented model and doll classification and labeling. Its six working areas cover category boundaries, adult versus child-like distinction metrics, service life and hygiene, compliant search terms, mandatory 18+ labeling, and legal-counsel plus ethics-review requirements. A group standard is a trade-level document rather than a national law, but it sets the vocabulary and testing yardstick that enforcement can later reference.

The AI companion rules. The Interim Measures for the Administration of AI Anthropomorphic Interactive Services took effect July 15, 2026. They ban virtual companion and intimate-relationship services aimed at minors; require user control over interaction data; restrict sensitive personal information from being used for training without consent; and require intervention for extreme emotional states, anti-addiction reminders and usage-time alerts. These rules govern the chat and personality layer rather than the doll hardware alone.

Open ethics. Beyond the minor-form ban, the unresolved questions are privacy of conversation, memory and any camera or sensor data; emotional dependency and social withdrawal; gender stereotyping; likeness rights for custom or clone faces; materials hygiene and quality control; and age-boundary enforcement on sales platforms. None of these is specific to China, but the makers' disclosures on them are generally thinner than on the hardware itself.

What this means for overseas readers

For buyers, importers and analysts watching the category, the Chinese advantage is the physical base: materials, molds, handcraft, electronics integration, price and customization breadth. The AI layer is where US and European brands still lead on software maturity, brand and service. Most Chinese AI dolls are export-first; domestic demand exists, but marketing and sale operate under restricted rules, so a domestic product launch would have to clear both the adult-products labeling frame and the July 2026 AI companion rules.

The honest read for a global reader: this is a hardware export category quietly absorbing robotics and voice AI, not a mainstream consumer-robot segment yet. The supply chain is real and large; the AI software is young; the regulatory frame is being written in real time.

What to watch

  • The Guangdong group standard publication. Whether the adult versus child-like metrics, 18+ labeling and hygiene requirements become enforceable references, and whether other provinces follow.
  • A domestic Chinese-language AI launch. Whether WMdoll or another maker releases a compliant domestic version, and how it handles age verification and marketing language.
  • Price convergence. Whether Chinese AI lines move upmarket toward Realbotix pricing, or US and European brands source Chinese hardware behind their own brands.
  • Data rules applied to doll chat. Whether makers disclose cloud versus on-device speech, conversation retention and third-party model sharing, under the July 2026 AI companion measures.
  • Platform compliance. Age verification, likeness-rights handling and listing language on cross-border trade platforms.

Frequently asked questions

Which Chinese companies make adult-oriented robot dolls?

The best-documented names are WMdoll (金三玩美) in Zhongshan, whose MetaBox series added servo motion, tactile sensors and LLM voice conversation, and EXDOLL, a 2009-founded high-end silicone doll house with 3D-scan and 3D-print cloning lines. A wider Zhongshan and Dongguan cluster — including cited names such as Chunshuitang, Junying and IronTech Doll — supplies static dolls and OEM/ODM units to overseas brands. Many smaller traders list private-label units on B2B platforms.

How much do Chinese AI doll robots cost?

China Daily reported in March 2025 that WMdoll's first MetaBox batch sold in North America, Japan and Europe at US$1,600–2,000, with high-end models near US$3,000, and that a full-function AI subscription started at around US$100 for the first year. Static TPE and silicone dolls sit in the thousand-USD band; robotic and AI-equipped models run above it. The US benchmark Realbotix listed dealer snapshots in September 2026 at about US$7,350 for a robotic head and about US$11,350 for a full robot.

Are these products legal to sell in China?

Adult products may be produced and sold in China when they comply with applicable rules, but sexualized depictions of minors are a strict legal red line under minors-protection and obscenity statutes. In late 2025 local authorities investigated child-like dolls and paused production. A Guangdong group standard on classification and 18+ labeling was initiated in December 2025, and July 2026 rules on AI anthropomorphic interactive services cover the chat and companion layer, including a ban on virtual intimate services for minors.

How do Chinese dolls compare with RealDoll / Realbotix?

Realbotix, which grew out of Abyss Creations' RealDoll line in San Diego, positions a handcrafted artisanal product with a mature AI companion system — the Harmony servo head with personality traits and conversation memory, and the 17-motor Aria silicone face — at roughly five to seven times the price of a Chinese AI line. Chinese makers compete on materials, molds, handcraft cost, electronics integration and customization breadth; their software maturity, brand and after-sales service are younger. Most Chinese AI lines launched overseas before a domestic Chinese-language version.
